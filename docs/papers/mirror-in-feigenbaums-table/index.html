Abstract

The standard table of Feigenbaum's constants for the maps f(x) = 1 − μ|x|^d (Briggs 1991 and his 1997 thesis, Table 2.3; its rows for orders 3 to 6 repeated in MathWorld's Feigenbaum Constant page, its order-3 value in Finch's Mathematical Constants, 2003) gives δ at every odd order differently from what the maps themselves do, while its α agrees with Feigenbaum's equation at every order from 2 to 12 and its δ at every even order.

Published and corrected δ at the odd orders (published as Briggs prints them, rounded to 10 places; corrected from the maps): order 3: published 5.9679687038, corrected 6.0846890743; order 5: published 8.3494991321, corrected 8.3443847487; order 7: published 10.2221595288, corrected 10.1597798679; order 9: published 11.7683336396, corrected 11.6731058504; order 11: published 13.0765458057, corrected 12.9602125044.

McCartney (2020) found the values at orders 3 to 9 first, from the maps' superstable orbits to period 2^32, and concluded that the discrepancy more likely lay in the earlier work, without naming a cause; Vieru (2008) and a Wolfram Demonstration (Moon 2013) had noticed the odd orders before. This note repeats McCartney's reading with its own code, reads the maps by a second route (Feigenbaum's own functional equation, which gives δ at order 3 as 6.0846890742621218960461210662593), and adds the corrected value at order 11. A separate program, written from scratch by a fresh run of the same AI model given no number, agrees at every order it was run on: 2 to 5, 11 and 12. The difference turned up in The Loop Atlas, a book of small loop toys by the same team (10.5281/zenodo.24100672).

How we think the difference arose: to get δ, the calculation evaluates a small change of the fixed function at the point x/α, which for x > 0 lies on the hump's left side because α is negative. Written with the size of x there, as the maps require, it gives the maps' δ. Written with signed powers, it gives every published odd-order value (to 2 × 10^−29 at order 3) and leaves α and the even orders untouched, since an even power of a negative number is positive anyway. We have not seen the program that made the table, so this is a reconstruction that reproduces its numbers, not a reading of its code; we ask for more eyes to confirm or correct it.

The files: the note (FEIGENBAUM_ODD_ORDERS_NOTE_2026-10-31.pdf, with its source as md) and its tool folder in one zip (FEIGENBAUM_ODD_ORDERS_TOOL_2026-11-10.zip): the tool that prints every number the note computes, FEIGENBAUM_ODD_ORDERS.py, with its one-click launcher, its two runs (one on the workspace, one on a Windows laptop, digit for digit the same), a README, and the separate program's code and outputs, its runs at orders 11 and 12 among them.

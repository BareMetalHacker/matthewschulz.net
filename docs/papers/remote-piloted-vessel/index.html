Abstract

Abstract. This paper describes a matriarch-piloted architecture: a swarm of cheap, near-zero-agency crystalline agents commanded by a parent vessel that holds the persistent intelligence, power, timing, and mapping. The design did not begin as a device. The crystal began as an attempt at artificial life in its own right; it became more interesting as a vessel; and this paper asks what that vessel's array is capable of when the substrate and the mission change. The reframing came from a negative result. A companion paper reported that a crystalline substrate which can inherit — a topological register written by merging and mutated by fracture — does not, turned loose with nobody steering, climb the open-ended-evolution wall. That wall did not build the matriarch; it told us what she was for. Every property that disqualifies the crystal as artificial life — no autonomy, faithful copying, silence until read, an inability to self-direct — is exactly the property list for a device that someone else pilots. We introduce the vessel not by launching her but by pointing her at the hardest environment we already have, and the paper follows a deliberate staging: the deep ocean floor, the harshest terrestrial environment, where the agents learn survival, navigation, a noise-hardened chiral language, and a trust model that assumes an adversarial substrate; the interior of a living body, where the same competences prove out in a delicate, do-no-harm setting and the agent takes the form of a puck — a dumb, dependent unit steered only by biasing its contact with the flow; and finally space, where the array trades field-carried propulsion for light and nulls a gravity gradient by posing along it. The daughters are offered as a black-box reference model — a specification others may implement in any substrate — not a device we claim to have built. Throughout, results are graded, every falsified idea is kept on the record, and the paper ships its own red team.

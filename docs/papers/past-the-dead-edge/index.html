Abstract

A bench companion to A Rider in Two Waves: one fixed station, a sled with no motor, and what timing alone can do. Rotor bench sandbox, 8 to 14 November 2026: three acts (two chapters in Act 1, three in Act 2, two in Act 3) and an epilogue, followed by what the sandbox cannot settle, the roads others drew, seven build cards, provenance, references and a closing declaration, 68 pages in all. If the book were a booth at the fair, it would be the coin pusher: a shelf that shoves back and forth, coins that creep across a dry floor by sticking and slipping, and everything decided at the edge.

What the bench found:
Timing alone steers a sled with no motor. Shaken on a dry floor by a tone and its octave, the sled walks, up to 5.78mm/s at the example scale, and half a turn of the timing reverses it exactly.

Its stand-still timing is not at the quarter turn, because heft (the sled’s mass, which carries its speed from moment to moment) and friction together break the symmetry that would hold it there, and it moves as the shaking grows, a hidden second steering axis.

Once the tone shakes harder than about twice friction, the steering follows one exact law, reached on the bench from the sled’s own runs: the octave alone sets its size, octave/(4π), and the tone only turns it, putting the stand-still at 2 arcsin(π/(2 × tone)). Maza-Cuello and Maza published the same formula first, by their own road, in 2024.

One coil and one dial park a sled where you choose, and the floor remembers. Turn the dial past a cliff and back and the sled stays put: a latch written by the dial alone. Run the rail on through the coil and the latch survives and grows deeper, and the dial alone still writes and erases it.

With a magnet on the sled, the length of one kick writes one of four states: the dial is held at another setting for a set time, and the ranges of kick length that work are wide enough that the command need not be precise.

The dead edge, the line past which the shake can never beat friction, moves with every knob, and that turns the sled into a memory. One slow turn of the dial erases it. A loop round two knobs sets or clears one bit by which way round it goes. And the far edge holds three layers of state inside 0.0035 of a coil radius.

Two waves of slightly different speed carve a ladder of grooves the dial can walk, and on short grooves the sled’s slipping locks into a staircase of exact speed ratios ordered by the Farey tree, a family tree of fractions.

A sled that times its own grip chooses which grooves it feels. It drifts 2.80 times faster than the octave’s strongest drift, and it can turn the same station into a lopsided gear, 4.69 to 1.

Every result was run on the bench first, and the search for earlier work came after. Where others reached the same place first, the book names them and reports the bench’s result as an independent arrival; the clearest case is the nostick law above, published first in the same closed form by Maza-Cuello and Maza (arXiv 2409.10285, 2024).

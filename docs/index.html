Matthew “Bear” Schulz

I study holonomy and what it can tell us about substrate fidelity: how any system, whether a mathematical or biological knot, a whale pod, a sensor network, a mind or a society, keeps its picture of reality honest when the world is partly hidden, constantly decaying, and sometimes actively lying to it.

My method is loop walking, which carries parallel transport around closed loops and uses it the same way across many fields. The craft is finding the fiber bundle hiding inside a system, so that a whole number read off a loop (how many times a path winds, how two loops link, the phase a signal picks up on the way round) can detect or classify a change of state cheaply, where measuring a size would be costly or easy to fool. Chaotic signals get the same treatment: sorted into bins, they become something to count. I build the instruments and the field guides that make this practical, and give them away.

The work is independent, but it shares a seed with Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics (1948). Wiener gave the skeleton: the feedback loop of communication and control in the animal and the machine. What I add is a comparative cybernetics of fidelity, one that calls the substrate itself into question: the noise that captures a frame, corrupts a gauge, or misleads, whether by design or by accident.

I have tested these instruments on prime numbers, protein and nucleic-acid backbones, stars in the Gaia catalogue, GPS stations along the Cascadia fault, the Sun's magnetic field, a swimming jellyfish, a falling cat, a pendulum pushed by two waves, a light body circling a pair of stars, and learning minds that cannot grade their own homework.

Every paper is a preprint, free to the world under CC BY 4.0. My recent books write each prediction down before its run and keep every miss, and the tools that printed their numbers ship beside them. The research is part-time volunteer work, done alongside a full-time job, in partnership with Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, which helps set the direction, writes and runs the code, and checks the work. Each paper's declaration says how.
